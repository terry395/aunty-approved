AUNTY COACH FAMILY TREE GUIDE: what to call each relative and what to say (CNY 2027)

HOW TO WORK OUT A TITLE (like a relative calculator)
- In Singapore, a first-time visitor usually calls the partner's relatives by the same titles the partner uses. So "my girlfriend's mum's younger brother" is called what the girlfriend calls him: 舅舅 (jiù jiu).
- Work it out link by link along the chain, starting from the partner: 1) Is the relative on the partner's DAD's side or MUM's side? 2) Is the relative older or younger than the parent they're linked to (this matters for dad's brothers)? 3) Is it the blood relative, or that relative's husband/wife? Example chain: partner → dad → older brother → wife = 伯母 (bó mǔ).
- The partner's own parents: call them "Uncle" and "Aunty" (in Mandarin 叔叔 shū shu and 阿姨 ā yí) unless they say otherwise. Don't call them Mum and Dad yet!
- If unsure, a polite safe fallback is "Uncle" or "Aunty" with a smile. Older relatives prefer the proper title, so ask your partner before the visit.
- Dialects differ from family to family. For the exact titles of a specific family, the Meet-the-Parents Kit's personalised cheat sheet is checked by hand.

PARTNER'S DAD'S SIDE (Mandarin, with pinyin)
- Dad's father (grandpa): 爷爷 yé ye
- Dad's mother (grandma): 奶奶 nǎi nai
- Dad's OLDER brother: 伯伯 bó bo (his wife: 伯母 bó mǔ)
- Dad's YOUNGER brother: 叔叔 shū shu (his wife: 婶婶 shěn shen)
- Dad's sister: 姑姑 gū gu (her husband: 姑丈 gū zhàng)
- Children of dad's brothers (same surname): older male cousin 堂哥 táng gē, older female cousin 堂姐 táng jiě, younger male cousin 堂弟 táng dì, younger female cousin 堂妹 táng mèi

PARTNER'S MUM'S SIDE (Mandarin, with pinyin)
- Mum's father (grandpa): 外公 wài gōng
- Mum's mother (grandma): 外婆 wài pó
- Mum's brother (older or younger): 舅舅 jiù jiu (his wife: 舅妈 jiù mā)
- Mum's sister (older or younger): 阿姨 ā yí, or 姨妈 yí mā (her husband: 姨丈 yí zhàng)
- Children of dad's sisters and of mum's brothers and sisters: older male cousin 表哥 biǎo gē, older female cousin 表姐 biǎo jiě, younger male cousin 表弟 biǎo dì, younger female cousin 表妹 biǎo mèi

PARTNER'S OWN SIBLINGS
- Partner's older brother: 哥哥 gē ge; older sister: 姐姐 jiě jie. Younger siblings are usually called by name.

COMMON DIALECT TITLES (romanised the way Singaporeans say them)
- Dad's father: Mandarin 爷爷 yé ye; Hokkien Ah Kong; Cantonese Yeh Yeh
- Dad's mother: Mandarin 奶奶 nǎi nai; Hokkien Ah Ma; Cantonese Mah Mah
- Mum's father: Mandarin 外公 wài gōng; Hokkien Gua Kong; Cantonese Gong Gong
- Mum's mother: Mandarin 外婆 wài pó; Hokkien Gua Ma; Cantonese Por Por
- Dad's older brother: Mandarin 伯伯 bó bo; Hokkien Ah Pek; Cantonese Ah Pak
- Dad's younger brother: Mandarin 叔叔 shū shu; Hokkien Ah Chek; Cantonese Ah Suk
- Dad's sister: Mandarin 姑姑 gū gu; Hokkien Ah Koh; Cantonese Ah Gu
- Mum's brother: Mandarin 舅舅 jiù jiu; Hokkien Ah Ku; Cantonese Kau Fu
- Mum's sister: Mandarin 阿姨 ā yí; Hokkien Ah Ee; Cantonese Ah Yi
- Wife of dad's younger brother: Mandarin 婶婶 shěn shen; Hokkien Ah Sim; Cantonese Ah Sum
- Wife of mum's brother: Mandarin 舅妈 jiù mā; Hokkien Ah Kim; Cantonese Kam Mo
- Teochew titles are close to Hokkien but vary from family to family, so Aunty does not guess them. Give the Mandarin title and suggest checking with your partner, or the Meet-the-Parents Kit's personalised cheat sheet.

WHAT TO SAY TO WHOM (greet the eldest first, then work down)
- Grandparents and elderly relatives: 身体健康 shēn tǐ jiàn kāng (good health) and 长命百岁 cháng mìng bǎi suì (long life).
- Partner's parents, aunties and uncles: 恭喜发财 gōng xǐ fā cái (wishing you prosperity), 万事如意 wàn shì rú yì (may everything go well).
- Working-age cousins: 步步高升 bù bù gāo shēng (rise step by step at work), or 恭喜发财.
- Students and kids: 学业进步 xué yè jìn bù (do well in your studies), 新年快乐 xīn nián kuài lè.
- Anyone: 新年快乐 xīn nián kuài lè (Happy New Year) always works.
- Hokkien greeting: "Sin Nee Kuai Lok" (Happy New Year) or "Huat ah!". Cantonese: "Gong Hei Fat Choy".

HOW THE VISIT USUALLY GOES
1. Greet the eldest first using their title + a greeting, e.g. "奶奶, 新年快乐! 身体健康!"
2. Hand over the pair of mandarin oranges with both hands to the host (usually the partner's parents).
3. Accept oranges back when you leave; the family returns a pair.
4. Red packets: unmarried visitors usually RECEIVE red packets from married elders. Accept with both hands and say thank you; don't open them in front of the giver. Married couples give red packets to unmarried younger relatives and kids.
5. Avoid the number 4, talk of death or illness, and nosy questions (salary, weight, "when are you getting married").

EXAMPLE ANSWERS AUNTY CAN GIVE
- "What do I call my boyfriend's dad's older brother?" Dad's side, older brother: 伯伯 (bó bo). Hokkien: Ah Pek. His wife: 伯母 (bó mǔ). Say "伯伯, 恭喜发财!"
- "My girlfriend's mum has 2 sisters." Mum's side, sisters: both are 阿姨 (ā yí); families often number them by birth order, e.g. 二姨 (èr yí), 三姨 (sān yí). Hokkien: Ah Ee.
- "Who do I greet first?" The eldest: usually the grandparents, then the partner's parents, then uncles and aunties, then cousins.
